# Everything to bits

You own a dozen computers. This page is a deck of doors into them — a single self-contained HTML page, entered through the things computers already do for you, walked down to the sand at whatever pace curiosity sets. The climb underneath every walk is the authors' own guidance; the reader is never told it, and may put it together.

## The way in

- **The deck.** The landing page opens like the cover of a book: an overline, the title, a subtitle, and one paragraph naming the walk — then seven cards, each carrying its icon, its name, its question, and a small hint. No course, no lecture, no order. The reader picks a door.
- **The system.** Each card opens on a visual trail of the parts that make the thing happen, drawn as a vertical spine of tappable parts. Each part, tapped, explains itself in its own words and its own context: a short, concrete paragraph, no prerequisites.
- **The doors.** Every open step — part, trail step — ends in a row of round icons: doors into other paths, each wearing its target's icon and name, its question as a tooltip. Where a path is still to come, the circle is greyed and dashed but carries the missing path's own name, icon, and promise — a named placeholder, never filler. This is the one standard for leaving a path; blurbs carry no inline links, and nothing dangles at the bottom.
- **The ladder.** Every part carries a small label naming its rung of the universal ladder — *the world in, the world out, the manager, the program, the code, the machine, the dance, the fork, the bedrock, memory, the wire, time* — and parts always appear in ladder order, so every card walks the same descent from the world to the sand, whatever the thing.
- **The fundamentals.** Below the cards, one map of the territory beneath the stories, drawn from the model: the whole system authored once, as one structure — strata, nodes, and roads — that the rest of the deck is checked against, so a new view must first be placed in a stratum and its roads written there, and only then may a trail walk it. The strata run top to bottom, one zoom deeper each storey: *what a person sees* (the three sense trails of pictures, sound, and the camera, with the promises of the picture drawn, the press becoming a number, and the word becoming a place); *the boundary between the world and the data* (the measure trail, where the world becomes numbers, and the number-pushes trail, where numbers become the world: the crossing in both directions); *machines together* (the message, the satellite, the meeting of two hands, the many acting as one, the copies staying one, the failure, the survival); *one machine, at work* (the machine itself, the manager, the forks, the simulated worlds, the clock, the watchers, the secrets, closed by the promises of the game chip and the universal machine); *the words and numbers inside* (the written software and the facts, over the promises that keep, remember, route, learn, spell, and pay) — then the fork, the machine seen two ways, side by side: the abstract computers on the left (the Turing machine, recursion, the two rivers, the matching craft, the tower, time), and on the right the machine as built, opening into its components (the adder and the latch) and ending in the bedrock (the Bit and the gate). The left side bottoms in mathematics itself, nothing drawn beneath it; the components sit below the fork, not above it, because gates build adders and adders build the processor — the chain forces the bend. Heavier built-from lines with arrowheads spell the construction out, pointing upward from ingredient to built thing. The stories themselves are the cards above, not circles on the map. One line per road that descends exactly one storey, and only down — a road that climbs, runs level, or skips storeys is walked in the views, never drawn, so no line on the map ever rises; the fifty-five roads inside a storey, and the hundred and twenty long walks, stay in the views. Every circle opens its view, and hovering one lights the full path down from it — the landings beneath, and the construction they stand on, as one connected route of tracks: the drawn lines between lit circles light, and the lit steps the map leaves undrawn, its long walks, are traced live, dashed and in the accent colour, only while hovered, so the static map keeps its rule of walked-not-drawn and the hover still shows the whole way down — and never anything above the hovered circle; a circle with nothing beneath it, a promise or the bedrock itself, rings alone without dimming the map. The model is also the deck's canon of order: the trails page walks the strata top to bottom, each stratum in its authored order. A sticky bar keeps *back* and *deck* one tap away from anywhere.

## The climb

Computation writ large, the one shape every answer shares: the world presses in and sensors turn it into numbers (input); programs decide what to make of the numbers, a manager shares the machine among them, and the machine obeys stored instructions, one at a time, on the beat (transformation); the decisions bottom out in logic gates and the numbers in bits (bedrock); and the result is turned back out into the world, in time (output). Three more rungs cut across the climb: memory (holding numbers against time), the wire (handing numbers between machines), and time (two hands, one instant).

This description is the authors' own guidance, kept as data and never rendered. There is no pattern page: the deck starts at the cards, and the reader drills down. The ladder is data only — one ordered list of slim rungs, an id and a chip each, the rails merged in as ordinary rungs — whose order sorts the parts of every card and whose chips label them. The reader walks the same descent many times and may put the shape together; nothing spells it out.

## The layering

The story the deck tells is that the taken-for-granted things of everyday life are carried out by computers doing the basic things computers do, and that this builds, layer on layer, into the high-level experiences of technological society. The reader should be able to walk from any story down to the sand in small steps, each step one idea. Where the walk used to leap — from *a list of instructions* straight to *a number that means a command*, from *the laws of the air spelled as arithmetic* straight to fetch–decode–execute, from *a warehouse of computers* straight to the one machine — the deck now has a trail in between, one layer under the stories and one above the basics: **how software is written** (words to numbers), **how a world is simulated** (model to machine), and **how many machines act as one** (warehouse to box). The primers are reached from trails, and the four loneliest primers — recursion, the two rivers, the matching craft, and the tower — had no incoming doors at all until the written-software trail gave them their first. The trails now descend by degrees too: their first half opens high, on trails and promises, never straight down to the sand, which is reached only in the second half, on the way to the landing — and the build enforces the rule, so a front step can no longer leap to the basement. The earliest steps are carried by real trails now: *how anything becomes a number* is the measure trail, the first way on from the sense trails, and its mirror *how a number moves a thing* is the last step of every other trail, the number leaving the machine; one promise still waits up there, *how one machine can be any machine* (the universal promise, the first way on from the machine trail). The deck's own honesty has a family now too: *how everything fails* (the law that no system works perfectly, and the safeguard that fails by protecting), *how the whole survives* (walls, watchers, and the crash treated as a message), and *how the copies stay one* (many machines wearing one name, led by whoever is alive and noticed) stand in the middle layer between the always-there services of the cards and the one machine the primers explain.

## How it is built

One file arrives at the reader: self-contained, no framework, no network. Behind that file sits a small build: the deck data lives in seven schema-governed XML parts under one directory — icons, primers, placeholders, cards, trails, ladder, and the model (the whole territory: strata, nodes, and roads, the map and the built-from chain subsumed) — each carrying one root element and validatable alone, the template holds the prose and the engines, and one command validates, builds, tests, and installs — the published page is build output, never hand-edited. The page is designed the way LISP and FORTH would design instructional software: structure and content are split, and the structure is data walked by four small words.

- The deck kernel: the data — cards, parts, blurbs, doors, rung tags, the named placeholders, the twenty-one trails, the ladder, and the model (the strata and their fork, the nodes, and the roads: land where a trail bottoms out, cross where a step crosses, build where a thing is built from another) — lives in seven XML parts governed by one XSD schema. A part and a step are prose and doors, nothing else; a rung is an id and a chip, nothing else. The diagrams that once rode along are gone, and the interactive figures live in the template, never the data. The schema is the single source of truth: the build validates the data against it before rendering anything (the validator is a small interpreter for the exact XSD subset the schema uses, since the environment offers none). Doors render as round icons wearing the target view's icon and name, at least two per open step; where a path is still to come the door points at a named placeholder, which renders as a greyed, dashed circle carrying its own name and icon — never anonymous filler. One `doorHTML` walks every row, dedupes targets, and pulls labels and icons from the views and the placeholders themselves; the trails' doors also draw the map on the landing page, which reads the model: every view beneath the stories a circle in exactly one labelled stratum, one line per road that descends exactly one storey — never upward — roads inside a stratum and the walks that climb or skip storeys walked rather than drawn, the built-from roads drawn as heavier arrowed lines between the bottom storeys, and the stories kept off the map — they are the cards. The build refuses a view in two strata, a view in none, a stratum id used twice, a story drawn as a node, an edge that leads nowhere, a road the territory has twice, a landing that climbs, a door a trail opens that the model has no road for, a model that never forks, one that opens the fork with the wrong side, one that closes the fork early, and a built-from edge that skips a storey, crosses the fork, or touches a promise. The bottoms keep their closing note and end in their authored doors.
- The deck is layered, and the layers are data: cards carry level 0, trails and level-1 placeholders level 1, primers level 2 — pinned in the schema by enumeration. The reader descends gradually: a card's doors lead to trails, placeholders, or other cards, never straight to a primer, and a trail starts high and descends by degrees — its first half never doors a primer, the sand being reached only in the second half, on the way down; the build rejects a jolt and a first-half leap with the exact row and target, and also insists that every part and step carry at least two distinct doors and that every placeholder be referenced.
- The landing page opens like the cover of a book: an overline, the title, a subtitle, one paragraph naming the walk, seven cards each carrying its icon, name, and question, and — under the fundamentals — the map of the territory beneath the stories. Four builders walk it all (`renderDeck`, `renderSystems`, `renderExplainers`, `renderFundDAG`) over two shared primitives (`buildStrand`, `buildBottom`); `renderSystems` sorts every spine into ladder order, so a card's parts can be authored in any order and still descend; `renderExplainers` walks the strata of the model in their order, so the trails page reads the territory top to bottom, each stratum in its authored order; and `renderFundDAG` draws the strata straight from the model: each storey a labelled capsule of circles in authored order, then the fork as two columns drawn side by side — the abstract computers on the left, closing on a floor that says the mathematics beneath them is drawn by no one, the machine as built on the right — one line per road that descends exactly one storey, never upward, greyed and dashed where the target is still a promise, and the built-from roads as heavier arrowed lines — each leaving the ingredient by the top of its circle and hooking into the rim of the circle it builds, past the channel its name leaves free. A traced walk anchors on the facing rims of its two circles, so it never crosses an icon, whichever way the walk runs. Hovering any circle lights the full path down from it — its landings first, then the construction they stand on, completed as one web once any part of it is reached — and dims the rest; nothing lights above the hovered circle, and a circle with nothing beneath it, such as the Bit, rings alone. The module is layered so the hover is tested as logic before it is wired: a pure decision layer computes what a hover lights, split into the drawn lines and the undrawn walks it must trace; an application layer turns one decision into class toggles and dashed paths; the wiring listens to the mouse. The decision layer is exercised whole by `tests/hover.js`, a dependency-free harness that lifts the built data, checks every circle's route — down-only, connected, honest bottoms — against an independent reimplementation of the contract, and runs `applyHighlight` against a mock of the few DOM calls it makes. The map is a module of its own, `fund.js` and `fund.css`: one source in two builds, for the deck build inlines it into the template at markers, and the isolated build loads the same files whole, with the same shared helpers and the same data, to exercise the map and its hover alone. The view stack is three words (`go`, `goBack`, `homeDeck`), a FORTH-style trail of views with the deck at the bottom. Adding a card is adding an XML element with rung-tagged parts; adding a trail is adding another plus a view in its band; the deck grows both directions without new code.
- The pipeline: one command validates each data part against the schema — a part alone on request, all seven assembled by default — cross-checks the data (door targets, rung tags, icon names, the layered descent, door floors, placeholder references, the roads of the model — every edge leads somewhere, the territory has each road once, a landing never climbs, every door a trail opens is a road the model has, and each built-from edge spans one storey without crossing the fork — and the hover contract: every trail, hovered, must reach at least one primer, and every circle's route — the drawn lines it lights plus the walks it traces — must connect the whole way down), inlines the map module into the template, and checks the built page itself — its title, its views, its balanced tags — before installing. Three options serve the work: the data-only run (validate and check everything, render nothing), the part run (schema-check one XML part alone), and the isolated map build (the module, its data, and a per-circle hover report, on a page of their own). The logic of the hover is tested separately, at the level of the decision layer, by `tests/hover.js`. The checks are few and deliberate, each guarding a mistake that has actually happened; a failure prints every problem found, names the exact row and target, writes nothing, and the canvas is written only after all checks pass. The live figures in the primers live in the template and are not driven by the build.
- One table of gate definitions (`GATES`) computes every truth table and every wire colour on the page.
- One shared transport player (`makePlayer`) runs the four machines — the Turing machine, the teaching CPU, the recursion demo, and the Prolog search — with play (toggling to pause), step, and stop.
- The FORTH calculator, the LISP cons-cell sketchpad, the Prolog pond, and the race track are small engines over a shared handful of helpers.
- The Prolog section is a real mini-resolver: unification with variables renamed per rule use, backtracking with choice points, every attempt on the record (long runs of rejections fold to one line).
- The concurrency figure is one state machine in two postures: two workers racing on an unowned cell, then the same workers handing +1 through a pipe to a single owner — the lost update and its cure, both steppable by hand.
- The script is written in numbered sections; the CSS has one labelled block per figure plus one for the deck and systems. The view-source paragraph keeps itself honest with what the page actually contains.

## Working conventions

- Prose voice: short declaratives, long clause-linked sentences, committed claims, no hedges, no filler. Curly quotes and em dashes throughout.
- Structure and content are split: the structure is visual and mobile-first (a spine of tappable parts, one thing per screen, a sticky way back), and the content arrives only on interaction — the reader asks, the part answers, in its own context.
- Curiosity first: the reader is brought to the fundamentals, never spoon-fed them. Each part must represent a question a real person has about a thing they really use, and each interaction must make one small idea sink deeper. No epigraphs, no surveys of the ground to be covered.
- Novice-first, by design: every piece of notation is read aloud where it first appears, the prose says what to do with each interactive figure before the figure appears, and where the reader can predict an outcome (Zeus's grandparents, 1011 plus one) the prose invites the prediction first. Sidenotes carry the history; the running text carries the explanation.
- No tropes for tropes' sake: the point is to wake the reader to the road less taken, so each topic is explained by what it *means* and what it *costs*, not by the shape it wore in the last textbook.
- Sidenotes are numbered; history and citations live there, not in the running text.
- Doors are round icons, uniformly: every open step ends in a row of at least two round doors into other paths, each labelled with its target's icon and name; where a path is still to come the door is a named placeholder — greyed and dashed, carrying the missing path's own name, icon, and one-line promise. Never an empty placeholder; no inline links inside blurbs, no dangling links at the bottom, no exceptions.
- The climb is guidance for the authors, not a lesson for the reader: it lives as data (the ladder, rung tags, ladder ordering in the XML) and no page shows it. The deck starts at the cards, and the reader drills down; walking the same descent many times is left to do its own teaching.
- Every historical or technical claim is verified before it is written.
- Deck content is entered as XML against the schema; the schema changes first when the data's shape must grow.
- The deck data is checked by the build before anything is written: schema, references, descent, floors, and the built page itself, all in one Python script with no dependencies. The live figures in the template are exercised by hand; the build does not drive them.

## Growth plan

The deck is built to grow; every layer takes new content without disturbing the others. A new card is now a filled-in scaffold, not a blank page: pick the thing, write its concrete parts, tag each part with its rung, and the spine, the chips, the doors (with named placeholders until real doors exist), and the ladder ordering all assemble themselves. Planned, one vertical at a time:

- **Hovering helicopters** — the gyroscope and pressure sensors (world in), the rotor told a number called throttle (world out), the flight controller (manager), the autopilot's hold-this-height promise (program), the measure-compare-correct loop (code), the little brain (machine).
- **Drone choreography** — a swarm holding a shape: positions sensed and shown, one shared moment among many dancers, the wire keeping the copies in step.
- **More trails, now promised in the data.** Four greyed circles carry the deck's next trails, each pointed at by the rows that raise it: *how money moves* (one ledger, no double spend, no one in charge — the rider and the wire), *how the picture is drawn* (triangles, a pipeline, a thousand small painters — the game's own arithmetic), *how everything is kept* (tables, and the shortcuts that find them again — the warehouse's parked orders and the ground room's day of sky), and *how a machine improves its guesses* (guesses scored, and the better ones kept — the suggestions beyond search). Fleshing any one out is writing its trail, deleting its placeholder, and re-pointing its doors at the new view; the simulated-worlds promise was fulfilled exactly this way, the measure and number-pushes promises the same way, back to back, and the facts and race promises the same way after them, one grounded in the matching craft and one in the meeting of two hands, and the camera, clock, and watching promises the same way after that: the picture's missing half, the GPS card's beat, and the watchers' eyes, and the secrets promise the same way after them: the locked box opened at last, by mixing, signatures, and vouching.
- **New primers.** Compilation is no longer a promised primer: the written-software trail now carries it down to the machine's own numbers, one layer above the basement. Deeper primer plans stay: C, memory, pointers, the malloc/free bug taxonomy ending at Rust's ownership; and types as safe decoding. Let-it-crash is no longer a distant primer: the survival trail carries it now, as a whole step of its own.
- **Card-first additions:** anything fundamental enough to earn a card question gets a card, not a chapter.

## Discipline for extensions

Each primer: a few paragraphs, one live figure, one or two sidenotes, an ending that points outward. Each card: real things people do, parts that explain themselves alone, every part tagged with its rung, doors down that mean something. Every new destination earns a question phrased the way a curious reader would ask it. Keep figures and sidenotes numbered in order; keep the ladder, the fundamentals map, the system trails, and the view-source paragraph honest with what the page actually contains.
