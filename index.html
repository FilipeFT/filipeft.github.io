Everything to bits

Something your computers already do, walked all the way down to the sand.

A computer is a machine that follows instructions, exactly and at absurd speed. There is nothing else in the box. Each card opens on a thing one of your computers already does for you, and each trail walks it down, part by part, until it stands on the sand. The trails branch, so one question leads to another at your own pace. Follow them as far as curiosity carries you, and step back to the deck whenever you like. There is no order here and no course, though whatever the thing, every walk has the same shape.

The Bit

A computer is a machine of two states. A voltage is high or it is low. A switch is closed or it is open. A magnet points north or it points south. The two states are written 0 and 1, and each such digit is called a bit. That is the entire alphabet. Everything else, numbers, music, this sentence, is arrangement. The machines you own (the phone, the console, the television) are built of nothing else.

Below, five switches. Click them and count.

The places are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, each twice the one to its right. The numeral 10101 is not a mystical string; it is 16 + 4 + 1 = 21 written in the only alphabet the machine has.1

1Binary numerals are older than computers by a comfortable margin: Leibniz worked the arithmetic out in 1679 and admired how neatly it expressed creation — everything out of nothing, one sufficing.

One bit says little. Two bits can be related: both are 1, at least one is 1, they disagree. A device that answers such questions about its inputs is a logic gate, and it has a primer of its own.

The Old Master said: ten thousand programs, two states, one switch. Understand the switch plainly and the ten thousand hold no surprises.

Gates: the whole vocabulary

A gate is a few pieces of carefully poisoned sand, a transistor or four, that answers one question about its input bits. Six kinds are worth knowing. The wire below turns red when it carries a 1; the pads take clicks, and so do the buttons.

XOR answers exactly one of you? — addition with the carrying withheld, 1 + 1 leaves 0 and a debt. NAND, not both, is the gate physics likes best; it goes to work in the adder primer.2

2De Morgan’s laws are the working hand’s pocket knife: invert and swap AND↔OR — not (a and b) is (not a) or (not b). This is why one gate can impersonate all the others, and why you only ever need to stock one part in the parts bin.

Real gates are not instant. Signals take picoseconds to cross, and the sand runs warm. The latch, the clock, the ripple of the adder further on: all of it engineering with that sloppiness rather than against it. From here on, though, ideal gates — honest, immediate, tireless.

One gate to rule them all — and then, arithmetic

The gate primer’s toolbox is overstocked: one gate is enough. NAND alone can impersonate all the others. Tie its two inputs together and it becomes NOT. Feed a NAND’s output to both inputs of a second NAND and you get AND. By De Morgan’s rules, three NANDs make an OR. The tables below are computed live by the same gate definitions that draw Fig. 2:

Now arithmetic. Add two bits a and b: the answer’s ones-column is XOR(a,b), and the carry is AND(a,b). That two-gate gadget is called a half adder — half, because it can only add two bits; it has no place to receive the carry arriving from the next-lower column. The full adder repairs the omission: it adds three one-bit numbers at a time (a, b, and the incoming carry), which takes two half adders glued together by an OR gate.

Chain four full adders, feeding each carry into the next, and you can add any two numbers from 0 to 15. The carry ripples from the 1s column to the 8s, exactly like the little 1s you carry when adding by hand:

Somewhere inside the processor in front of you, a wider and faster cousin of this pattern is adding numbers billions of times a second. Arithmetic did not grow more mysterious as it grew real; it grew more parallel.

A loop of wire that remembers

Everything so far has a flaw: it is stateless. Change the inputs and the output follows; nothing persists. A machine that holds no state cannot count, cannot iterate, has no before and no after. The remedy is a loop: feed a gate’s output back into its own input, and time enters the circuit.

Cross-couple two NOR gates, each watching the other’s output, and you have the SR latch:

While S and R are both 0, ask the latch “why is Q 1?” and it answers: “because Q̄ is 0. Why is Q̄ 0? Because Q is 1.” The state is a loop of mutual justification, a tiny strange loop.3 Add a gate in front to decide when the latch may listen, and a clock, a steady pulse of billions of beats per second, to do the deciding, and you have a flip-flop: memory that changes only on the beat. Put 64 of them side by side and you have a register. This is all “memory” ever was: loops with a doorman.

3Douglas Hofstadter would want the phrase back: Strange Loops is his, and the latch is the smallest hardware instance of the phenomenon — a system whose output, chased far enough, circles back to become its own premise.

Gates compute and latches remember. What is missing is purpose, a machine that carries out a plan, and the Turing primer holds the plan in its purest form.

In 1936, before any electronic computer existed, Alan Turing asked what “mechanical procedure” could possibly mean, and distilled it to almost nothing:4

  • an endless tape of cells, each holding one symbol;
  • a head that reads and writes one cell at a time, and moves left or right;
  • a finite state of mind, and a fixed table of rules: “if I am in state q and I see symbol s, write t, move, and switch to state q′.”

That is the entire machine. No numbers, no arithmetic, no addresses. Given tape and time it computes anything any computer can compute — anything at all: a phone and a supercomputer are, in Turing’s sense, the same object.

A real machine: the von Neumann computer

Turing gave the concept; von Neumann’s 1945 architecture gives the shape every processor still wears. The idea is nearly a joke: take the Turing machine and fix the tape in place (call it memory, a row of numbered cells holding numbers), and hard-wire the rule-following into a circuit called the processor. Two registers are exposed to the programmer: a program counter (PC) holding the address of the next cell to obey, and an accumulator (ACC) to compute in. A third, the instruction register (IR), is the machine’s hand: it holds the number most recently fetched while the decoding gates stare at it.

The processor knows one dance, the fetch–decode–execute cycle:

repeat forever:
  fetch    IR ← memory[PC]        -- copy the next cell into the instruction register
  decode   "what does this number mean?"  -- 1xx = load, 2xx = add, …
  execute  do it                   -- and usually PC ← PC + 1

The radical part: the cell holding 108 is an instruction only because PC pointed at it. The very same memory holds the data. Code is numbers. Below, a complete little computer with ten cells of memory and eight instructions; it counts down from five, printing each value. Step it slowly:

Recursion, at ground level

The primers behind were preparation for one meeting: mathematics that seems to float free of the world, and the machine of sand that must run it. Recursion is where they recognise each other, because in a high-level language it looks perfectly abstract — a function defined in terms of itself, as if the machine did not exist:

(define (fact n)
  (if (= n 0)
      1
      (* n (fact (- n 1)))))

Read it aloud: the factorial of n is 1 when n is 0; otherwise n times the factorial of n − 1. True, elegant — and, if you have never met a processor, magical. Where does (fact 3) live while (fact 2) is being computed? Who remembers that the outer call still owes a multiplication?

But you have met a processor, and know the answer: frames on a stack. Each call gets a few memory cells, the frame, holding its own n and its place in the multiplication. A register, sibling of the PC, points to the top frame; calls push, returns pop. Below, (fact 4) becomes 24, the highlighted expression moving as it does:

The magic dissolves into particulars. “The call remembers its context”: some cells do. “The base case stops it”: the stack has a bottom. “Infinite recursion crashes”: the cells run out, and there is nothing mystical under stack overflow.6 Scheme itself is the fetch–decode–execute loop of Fig. 7 with two more instructions: call, push a frame and jump; return, pop it and jump back.

6Lisp (1958, John McCarthy) is the second-oldest language still in use, after Fortran. Its genius was to notice that programs are lists — data — and so programs could build programs. Turing’s blurring of code and data keeps reappearing at every level of the tower.

Two rivers, one sky: the minority view

The road most travelled runs through one machine, many layers, a tower of abstraction with a program at the top. It is the majority view, and it built the industry. A minority tradition, patient and mostly ignored, holds that a language need not be an empire, and that the two most honest languages ever designed are also the two smallest, mirroring one another as the sky mirrors the earth. They appeared a decade apart and face in opposite directions. LISP (1958) turned toward mathematics, meaning, the pattern behind things. FORTH (1968) turned toward the machine, the act, the cost of the act.

FORTH — the word that acts

In the 1960s, Charles H. Moore programmed computers for astronomy, and the trade became, in his hands, a decade-long act of subtraction. Every tool struck him as heavier than the work; every language, as a bureaucratic tax on thought. His answer was to make the language disappear almost entirely. By the time he was steering radio telescopes for the National Radio Astronomy Observatory, his system, which he called FORTH, was little more than the stack you met in the recursion primer, a dictionary of words, and a colon for teaching the machine new words from old ones.7

7The name was a pun: Moore meant “FOURTH,” as in fourth-generation language; the IBM 1130 he used permitted five-character names. The system had been distilling privately since the late 1950s and reached its essential form pointing at the radio sky — the 36-foot telescope at Kitt Peak among its first chores.

Numbers go onto the stack, words consume what they need and leave what they made, and : SQUARE DUP * ; teaches a new word, indistinguishable thereafter from a built-in. That is most of the specification. The whole language, on one desk:

Absent are variables, syntax trees, precedence rules, any border between the language and your program. The program is the dictionary, and the dictionary is the machine growing a vocabulary. Postfix order, the numbers first and the word last, is how the machine itself lives: fetch, fetch, act. FORTH is the machine primer without the apology.

LISP — the meaning that recurs

Five years earlier, pointed the opposite way, John McCarthy wanted a language in which a machine could reason about its own thoughts, and found that almost nothing is needed. The core he reached for was pure mathematics, Church’s lambda calculus, in which computation is nothing but functions calling functions: no machine, no storage, only substitution.8 From it LISP takes one data structure, the list. One way to build it: the pair or cons cell. A few primitives to take it apart. And the rule that a program is itself a list. Not like a list: is one. The catalogue and the catalogue’s reading instructions have the same shape.

8Lambda calculus — Alonzo Church, 1930s — was invented as pure mathematics, decades before anyone had built a machine to run it on. A list is a chain of pairs: each cell holds one thing and a pointer to the rest, and the last pointer points at nothing, nil. Three words are the hands for this material: (cons x …) attaches a new first thing, (car …) takes the first thing, (cdr …) takes the rest. The sky’s catalogue, for your hands:

The stack machine and the lambda calculus are the two minimal answers to Turing’s question, what is computation, and history has mostly chosen to build very large things atop each while visiting neither.

The columns are complements, not rivals, and an old Daoist shape appears between them. LISP is the language of the sky: pattern, recurrence, meaning growing by self-reference: recursion is celestial grammar, each cycle owing its shape to the cycle below it. FORTH is the language of the earth: the immediate word, the minimal gesture, direct contact with the stone, no representation standing between the intent and the act. The sky teaches what things are; the earth teaches what things cost. Neither is whole alone: a programmer who knows only meaning builds castles that cannot stand, and a programmer who knows only cost builds floors with nothing above them.

The secret identity

The recursion primer kept the proof in its pocket. To run recursion, the machine grew a stack of frames, each frame a note of what the suspended call still owed. FORTH is that stack promoted to an entire language; LISP is that recursion promoted to an entire language. Strip the notation and they are one machine: any program written in one can be rewritten, mechanically, as a program in the other: the sentence flattens into pushes and acts, and the pushes and acts, read again, curl back into a sentence. The middle of that translation has a name, the continuation, the meaning of “what to do next” made into a thing a program can hold, and the recursion primer was building continuations all along. The sky and the earth differ in posture, not in substance.10

10The rewriting is no parable; it is the first road of compiler construction. Plotkin (1975) gave continuations their calculus; Danvy (2003) showed the classic SECD machine of 1964 to be a continuation interpreter in disguise; Ghuloum (2006) showed how to grow such a compiler in small verified steps, each kept honest by tests. A reader who wants to watch the two rivers meet can follow that trail downhill, from sentence to stack.

The oldest computation

A telescope controller is the right place to meet all this, because the loop it closes is ten thousand years old. Astronomy was humanity’s first science, mathematics, and computation in one: people looked up, saw that the sky is the one place where the changing masks the unchanging, and discerned cycles — of days, of seasons, of years, of the slow recurrence of planets and the shock of a predicted eclipse.11 Recording the cycles forced the invention of number and of notation; predicting them was the first program execution.

11Babylonian base-60, a counting system inherited from Sumer and made immortal by the sky, outlived both: it survives in the degrees on a telescope mount and the minutes on your clock — thirty centuries old and still in daily use.

The second act of the same play: the earliest writing of Sumer and Egypt is not poetry or prayer: it is accounting. Tallies of sheep and grain pressed into clay; the token, the envelope, the tablet.12 The state was born as a ledger. Scribes were trained by copying the record and adding to it, generation onto generation, and the archive outlives the scribe exactly as the latch of the memory primer outlives the signal that set it, scaled up to a civilisation. Writing, teaching, remembering: culture itself as external memory. Civilisation formed as a form of abstract computation, the slowest and most successful information technology ever deployed.

12On Denise Schmandt-Besserat’s account, writing began as bookkeeping: small clay counting tokens (as early as 8000 BCE) became impressions on envelopes, impressions became marks on flat clay, and marks became script. Literature arrived later, and inherited the clerk’s tools.

The connections, stated plainly: the sky taught counting, the clay remembering, the scribal schools teaching. Computing is not a novelty of the last century; it is the oldest continuous human craft, order abstracted from recurrence, written down, passed forward, and the transistor only let the craft run at the speed of light. When Charles Moore pointed his five-character language at the radio sky, the loop quietly closed: the heavens that taught humanity to count were at last counted back through an instrument steered by a dictionary of a few dozen words.

Between the Tigris and the Euphrates, civilisation formed on silt carried from two directions. The minority view suspects that between LISP and FORTH, silt of a similar kind is being carried — and that when the historical relationship between society and software matures, it will build its cities there.

The third way: relations

LISP speaks in sentences, FORTH in single words. A third way of speaking appeared in Marseille, in 1972, and it speaks in facts. A Prolog program is a list of what is true (parent(gaia, cronus)., which reads aloud as gaia is a parent of cronus) together with a few rules about what follows from what. No instruction appears in it anywhere, no order of doing, nothing that says how: the programmer states the truths, asks a question, and the machine goes looking for the answer itself. FORTH asks what things cost, LISP asks what things mean; Prolog asks what things imply.

The notation is small enough to learn in a minute, and every mark in it earns its keep. A fact is a relation’s name, its subjects in parentheses, and a full stop to say this stands. A name that begins with a capital letter is not a name at all but a blank waiting to be filled: parent(gaia, X) says gaia is a parent of somebody, and the machine’s task is to find out who. A rule is a head and a body joined by :-, which reads as if; grandparent(A, C) :- parent(A, B), parent(B, C). says that A is a grandparent of C if A is a parent of B and B is a parent of C — the comma reads as and. And a question is any such sentence with ?- written in front of it: ?- grandparent(G, zeus). asks who is a grandparent of Zeus?, and the machine answers by filling in every G that the facts and the rules make true.

The finding is ordinary machine work, and the machine of the sand-up primers does it. Take the goals one at a time, try each matching fact in order, bind a variable where it fits, and when a road dead-ends, back up to the last choice and take the next. Unification and backtracking are the words. Nested loops are the act. Every language before made the programmer write the loops; Prolog keeps the loops in the machine, and the program holds only the truths. An algorithm is logic plus control, Kowalski’s equation, and this is the language that takes the split seriously.13

13Prolog was born of a project to make a computer reason in French: Alain Colmerauer and Philippe Roussel had a preliminary version running in Marseille at the end of 1971 and a definitive one at the end of 1972; the name, short for programmation en logique, was suggested by Roussel’s wife. Robert Kowalski of Edinburgh supplied the theory; J. Alan Robinson’s resolution principle (1965) supplied the engine — if a machine can search for proofs, then a list of facts is a program.

The subject matter is chosen on purpose. Hesiod’s Theogony, the first Greek cosmogony, is a genealogy of the sky: Uranus the sky and Gaia the earth beget Cronus and Rhea, and Cronus and Rhea beget Zeus. It is half myth and half table of facts, the exact shape Prolog wants. Nine facts and three rules hold all of it, a small pond of truths, and the figure below puts you beside the machine that swims in it. The chips at the top are questions. Then press play, or step slowly and read the search one line at a time: the pond lights the fact being tried, the search is the machine’s thoughts, and the answers gather at the bottom as they are found. Read the grandparent question closely. The machine walks every road that ends at Zeus, and the answer comes back twice over: Gaia and Uranus, the earth and the sky, each found once through Cronus and once through Rhea: four proofs of two answers, roads counted rather than destinations. The last question fails, and the failure is no malfunction: Chaos is Zeus’s ancestor but not his grandparent, and in Prolog a statement is true exactly when the machine can find a proof of it: a failed search is an answer, not an error. And the ancestor rule is the recursion primer’s subject: a relation defined in terms of itself, with the frames grown by the search, out of sight, the way LISP grows them, where FORTH would have made you push them by hand.14

14The third way stayed a minority taste, but it was bet on. Japan’s Fifth Generation project (1982–1992) wagered a decade of national research on logic programming as computing’s future, provoked answering programmes in America and Europe, and ended in quiet disappointment; Prolog survived it. IBM’s Watson parsed the questions it was asked in Prolog. Minority is not marginal.

A pond like this holds no time. The facts are timeless, the search is timeless, one question at a time; nothing in the language says now, or while, or first this, then that. What happens when two acts must share one world, the question the pond cannot even ask, is the time primer’s business.

Time enters

The sand-up machine was alone with its memory, and the loneliness was a kind of safety: one instruction at a time, one hand on the cell, order guaranteed by the clock. Most software lives in no such world. A server answers ten thousand strangers at once. A car runs a hundred little machines in parallel on one planet of wires. A phone keeps the network, the screen and the ear all in the air together. Time enters, and with it the oldest patient bug in computing: two acts, one memory, no agreed order. The memory primer hid the warning in the latch, two signals arriving without a doorman, release order deciding who wins, and a data race is that lesson scaled up: the forbidden state as an everyday occurrence.

The mechanism is small enough to hold in one hand. Two workers, one cell holding 5, and each worker running the three-line program the machine primer taught: read the cell, add one, write it back. Each worker owns its register, the cell belongs to no one, and between any worker’s read and its write, the other is free to do exactly the same:

Read the race as it ran: both workers read 5, both added one, both wrote 6, and the cell ended at 6 when honest arithmetic says 7: one increment vanished in the gap between a read and a write. Serialize the same steps by hand and the cell reaches 7, the bug disappears, and here is the cruellest property of a race: it passes tests. The unlucky order arrives only when no one is watching (under load, at scale, in the middle of the night), and the wrong answer is silent: a number slightly too small, a ticket sold twice, a balance that no longer balances.

The classic cure is the doorman of the memory primer, promoted to software: a lock around the read–modify–write, Dijkstra’s semaphore of 1965, so the second worker waits at the door until the first has left the room. It works, and it has carried fifty years of correct systems. But the doorman must be remembered on every path, requested in a consistent order, held briefly, released always; and one forgotten lock, one circular wait, one overlong hold is the forbidden state again, now in production.15

15Dijkstra introduced semaphores — the P and V operations, doormen in the strict sense — in “Cooperating Sequential Processes” (EWD-123, 1965), written while he built THE, an operating system presented together with a proof of its correctness: state the machine honestly, then show why it works.

The figure’s second mode takes the other road, and it is worth walking slowly, because nothing about the work changes (two workers, two increments, the same cell) and the bug is simply gone. The sharing has been taken away. The workers no longer touch the cell at all, because the cell no longer belongs to everyone; it has an owner, and the workers’ whole job shrinks to dropping +1 into a pipe, while the owner’s whole job is taking the messages one at a time. Step through any interleaving the scheduler cares to produce and the cell reaches 7, and the reason is worth a second reading. The race existed because two hands each held a copy of the cell’s story, and the copies could disagree. Here one hand holds the cell at every instant, and two stories cannot disagree where there is never a second story. The pipe is not a faster lock. It is the end of sharing.

Hoare gave this arrangement its calculus in 1978, Communicating Sequential Processes, in which a program is processes that cannot touch each other’s memory and can only hand values through channels, the handoff a rendezvous: the sender waits, and the message never belongs to the wire. Go (2009) built its channels on CSP and made the doctrine a proverb: “don’t communicate by sharing memory, share memory by communicating.” But the pipe, like the lock, is still a discipline, a promise kept by hand, and nothing in either language stops a tired programmer from reaching around it. Rust closes the gap by moving the promise into the compiler: every value has exactly one owner, and a program in which two workers can reach the same cell is not an unlucky program but a refused one. The race of this figure, in Rust, is not a wrong answer in the middle of the night; it is a red line at build time: the forbidden state made unsayable, caught in the dark of the compiler, years before it could ever have been found in production.16

16Hoare’s “Communicating Sequential Processes” (CACM 21:8, August 1978) proposed that input and output are basic primitives of programming; it later grew into a full calculus. Ownership, it turns out, is the whole of the cure; the doorman was only ever a stand-in for it. But a promise checked against memory needs a map of memory, which byte belongs to whom, where values live, when they die, and the sand-up machine kept memory a polite fiction: cells, numbered, nothing said about who owns them. The fiction has a famous repair (C, the heap, malloc and free, and the compiler that finally learned to read ownership), and it is a primer this deck has not yet built.

The tower, and how to build well inside it

The climb, end to end:

Each layer makes a promise, “you don’t need to know how I do it”, and that promise is what lets a single mind build at the top.17 But every abstraction leaks: latency leaks, overflow leaks, the forbidden state of the latch leaks. Ground-up understanding matters not because you will hold the whole tower in your head, but because a leak is less frightening when you can look down a floor.

17“All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky.” — Joel Spolsky, stating the occupational hazard of the tower. Feynman stated the repair procedure: create the layer once, and you own it forever.

Between the machine and the sky, the habits of good building. The machine discovered them before any engineer wrote them down:

  • Name a thing once, then use the name forever. The subroutine is the discovery, made at machine-code level, that a numbered cell can be called by name instead of copied. The scribes of Sumer were doing the same when forty sheep, once forty tokens, one per animal, became a numeral and a sheep sign — the number named once and reused for everything counted after; the whole history of software is the long war against saying the same thing twice.
  • Build the smallest thing that can possibly work. Turing’s tape is the pattern: the minimal machine, stated honestly, turns out to be enough for everything. Simplicity is not less care; it is care aimed at one target.
  • Add nothing until something asks for it. The von Neumann architecture refuses features until the program demands them; the machine that reached the Moon kept its programs in core rope, copper wire threaded by hand through magnetic cores (through for 1, around for 0), and its 4 KB of working memory was so tight that one address did seven jobs in a single flight.

Forth and Lisp are the two long-running experiments in taking those habits as an entire worldview: languages built the way Moore built his telescope controller and McCarthy his evaluator, from the smallest core that could suffice, extended only when the sky demanded it.

Seen that way, the two programs are one subject. The program in the machine is written, read, argued over and changed by people working together, and the shape of their working together ends up in the code: the paths people talk along become the seams the software keeps, and the borrowed rooms of one team fit or fail by the shape of another. The knowing, meanwhile, does not live in the text.
